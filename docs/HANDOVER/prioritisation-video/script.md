Two hundred and thirty-six projects. Six hubs. Seventy-eight weeks.

---

And one question that matters every year: which of them will actually finish by December?

---

A spreadsheet can rank projects. It can't tell you that. The answer depends on who is free, and
which lab chamber is open, week by week.

---

So the RPD planner works in two moves. First it decides what matters most. Then it plans that
against real capacity, and keeps re-planning as work moves.

---

Start with what matters. Every project is scored on thirteen dimensions.

---

They sit in five pillars. Strategic alignment. Regulatory and quality. Financial return. Market and
volume. And investment.

---

Each dimension gets a score from one to five against a written rubric. Regulatory compliance, for
example: one means none, five means a mandatory deadline inside six months.

---

The weights aren't equal. Strategy, regulation and finance count twenty-five each. Market and
volume count fifteen. CAPEX counts ten, and it's inverted: the cheaper the project, the higher it
scores.

---

Multiply, add up, and divide by the maximum of fourteen hundred. Take FV-500: seven hundred and
fifty-five points, fifty-four percent.

---

That percentage sets the band. Seventy and above is P1. Fifty-five is P2. Forty is P3. Anything
lower is P4.

---

But some things can't wait for a score. Three hard gates force a project straight to P1.

---

A regulatory deadline inside six months. A customer certification at risk, like Coke or Pepsi. Or
an active safety non-compliance.

---

The Coke protocol project scores sixty-nine percent, one point short of P1. Its certification gate
puts it there anyway.

---

Scoring doesn't change the plan by itself. The computed band sits next to the committed priority,
and you test changes in scenario mode first.

---

When you apply priorities, that's a deliberate, versioned step. Every version is kept.

---

Now the second move: planning against capacity. The scheduler lines every project up in a queue.

---

Priority comes first. Within a priority, work that's further along goes ahead. Projects in buyoff
and industrialisation before development, development before the queue. Then category, A-plus
first.

---

Each project has fourteen steps. Their durations come straight from Frigoglass's own lead-time
table. An A-plus project runs forty-four weeks. A category C runs eight.

---

Every step is one of three kinds. A design step books the project's engineer for each of its
weeks.

---

A lab step books a test chamber in the right region, and only if the chamber has a free platform.

---

An elapsed step, like CAPEX approval or the pilot, books nobody. It just takes calendar time.

---

The scheduler walks forward week by week and takes the first window where the resource is free. No
window inside seventy-eight weeks? The project is left out, and it says so.

---

Then every project lands in one of three outcomes. Within year, finished by week fifty-two.
Spillover, finished later. Or left out.

---

In the current run, eleven of forty-five finish within the year. Twenty-four spill over. Ten are
left out.

---

And the order works. Eighty percent of P1 projects finish this year. P4 and queued work wait.

---

It also shows you where the pressure is. Engineers are seventy-four percent loaded. Lab chambers
only thirty-eight. Design time is the bottleneck, not testing.

---

A plan is only right on the day it's made. So every stage of every project tracks its progress:
status, percent complete, actual start and end.

---

When the next run happens, finished stages become fixed history. In-progress stages keep their
start, and only the remaining weeks get scheduled.

---

A blocked stage holds the project until the block clears, and everything after it waits.

---

Updating progress never quietly reshuffles the portfolio. The project is flagged stale, and a
planner presses Recalculate when they're ready.

---

That creates a brand-new schedule version. The old one is never touched. You can always see what
the plan looked like before.

---

Health badges follow from the run. On track, at risk, off track, blocked or left out. Nobody types
them in, so nobody can dress them up.

---

So the cycle is: score, prioritise, schedule against capacity, track, recalculate.

---

Same inputs give the same plan, every time, and every change is in the audit log.

---

That's how Frigoglass gets an honest answer to what will be delivered this year, and can see
exactly why.
